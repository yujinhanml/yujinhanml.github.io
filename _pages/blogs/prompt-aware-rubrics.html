Better Context, Better Verifiers.

Challenges for Video Generation Verifiers / Reward Models. Compared with mathematics and coding, video generation presents two challenges. First, what makes a video “good” is often not clearly defined, particularly for aesthetics and preferences[1]. Second, clear, interpretable reward signals are difficult to obtain: two videos may both receive a score of 3, yet one looks visually appealing but fails to follow the editing instruction, while the other follows the instruction but contains local artifacts. The same score can reflect different problems and call for different improvements. An ideal verifier / reward model for video generation should therefore ground its judgments in specific task requirements and production evaluation criteria, providing accurate, interpretable judgments and rewards that make clear which requirements are satisfied and what still needs improvement.

We introduce Prompt-Aware Rubrics (PAR), a verifier (reward) framework for video generation. PAR starts by making the evaluation problem explicit: a Rubric Handbook defines the task dimensions that matter in production, and task-specific rubrics turn ambiguous overall quality judgments into binary questions with explicit criteria, interpretable outcomes, and answers that can be checked individually against video evidence. This connects reward signals to concrete requirements. The design draws inspiration from human evaluation experts: define what “good” means for the task, translate that definition into concrete checks, and train evaluators to apply those checks consistently.

PAR Components. Compared with visual reward models that directly predict overall preferences or scores along predefined dimensions, such as ImageReward[2], PickScore[3], and VideoReward[4], PAR implements the expert workflow as a Rubric Handbook, a Rubric Generator, and a trained Rubric Verifier. The handbook and generator construct better context for the verifier, turning open-ended evaluation into explicit, task-grounded questions. In the initial PAR version, the answering model uses a fast, JeV-style discriminative approach[5], returning only a 0/1 binary judgment for each rubric.

Compared with conventional reward-model designs, PAR’s core advantage is the more precise context, aligned with production evaluation criteria, supplied by its handbook and generator. This better elicits capabilities already present in the base model and allows PAR to scale more effectively as the answering model’s underlying capabilities improve.

PAR Evolution. PAR provides three versions: Single PAR → Agentic PAR V1 → Agentic PAR V4. All three share the same Rubric Handbook and Rubric Generator. The progression expands the Rubric Verifier from a single answering model into a reward system that combines multiple specialist tools and a workflow harness.

  • Single PAR uses one trained answering model to make fast binary judgments for each rubric.
  • Agentic PAR V1 combines the answering model with multiple specialist tools, producing evidence from multiple sources to improve PAR’s accuracy.
  • Agentic PAR V4 binds individual rubrics to multiple tools—lightweight operators, open-source models, and trained answering models—and organizes their evidence through a workflow harness for rubric-level judgment.

Improvements across the three versions on Bench2. The improvements in V1 and V4 highlight another advantage: rubrics can be bound to relevant tools in advance, with routing rules or a workflow harness organizing multi-tool calls. This enables multi-tool collaboration without first collecting expensive tool-calling training data to train a general tool-use policy.

Directions for Improving PAR. The current PAR answering model outputs 0/1 binary judgments for fast System 1 verification. For checks already within the base Rubric Verifier’s capabilities, the Rubric Handbook and Rubric Generator elicit those capabilities through better context, while training aligns its decisions with evaluation experts. Together, these changes improve System 1 accuracy. For capabilities the base verifier still lacks, such as recognizing a tiny malformed region, direct binary judgment remains unreliable. These tasks need a richer answering process, for example, CoT reasoning with tools such as cropping to reach a verdict (System 2).

The longer-term direction for PAR is therefore a hybrid verifier: System 1 makes fast, direct decisions for rubrics within the base model’s capabilities, while difficult rubric categories use System 2 for more elaborate chain-of-thought reasoning and tool-assisted inspection.

01 / THE IDEA

Motivation: building better context from human evaluation

Same model, different context

Consider an instruction with several editing requirements:

Change every red delivery cart in the courtyard to blue, add two trees near the gate, and preserve the camera motion, pacing, and duration.

A fixed, generic evaluation prompt might ask, “Does the edited video satisfy the user’s instruction?” The model must parse every requirement, decide where to look, compare the relevant regions, and compress its findings into one judgment. While attending to the overall scene, it may overlook whether the carts have been recolored as requested.

Now ask, “Have all red delivery carts in the courtyard been changed to blue?” The video and verifier are unchanged, but the context explicitly identifies the objects, attribute, and target change. In this example, the model can then identify the objects left unedited. This observation suggests that a missed failure does not necessarily imply a lack of basic atomic capabilities, such as color recognition; those capabilities may simply not have been effectively elicited. Improving context offers a way to elicit them.

This observation makes the optimization problem more concrete: how can we incorporate production evaluation knowledge into the context to better elicit a verifier’s atomic capabilities?

Borrowing the workflow of human evaluation experts

Consider how human evaluation experts assess generated videos. Experts first identify the dimensions that matter for a given generation task and document the criteria. Evaluators are then trained to consult that document and apply its standards consistently. A generic request to “judge the video” skips much of the work involved in human evaluation.

This human evaluation workflow inspired the Prompt-Aware Rubrics framework. PAR has three components: the Rubric Handbook stores expert knowledge as the generator’s system prompt; the Rubric Generator converts the user instruction into concrete checks, each of which should be an atomic question answerable with a binary verdict; and the Rubric Verifier answers those questions using video evidence and expert-aligned criteria.

What a prompt-aware rubric looks like

The Rubric Handbook contains a rubric pool. The Rubric Generator selects dimensions closely related to the current task and grounds them in the user prompt to construct rubrics. The pool has two levels: category and subcategory. The following fictional examples demonstrate this structure.

The table above shows a subset relevant to this task. The Rubric Generator selects applicable criteria from the categories and subcategories, then grounds its questions in the task; it does not need to draw questions from every category.

At inference time, the workflow is (handbook + editing instruction) → rubric generator → rubrics → answering model → rubric-level verdicts and an aggregate score. The answering model uses video evidence to make its decisions. In practice, we compute the normalized probability of “1” for each check and average these probabilities for the final score. Category weights can also reflect specific priorities, such as placing greater emphasis on consistency.

02 / THE SINGLE ANSWERING MODEL

PAR: a single answering model

Three stages of handbook refinement

The Rubric Handbook is the rubric generator’s system prompt, incorporating production evaluation standards. It has two parts: fixed generation requirements and a structured rubric pool.

The fixed requirements favor atomic checks, prioritize important requirements within a question budget, enforce consistent polarity, and discourage contradictions and duplicates. Consistent polarity means that a higher score always indicates better satisfaction of the corresponding task requirement. In our setup, rubrics use binary labels: a positive answer means the requirement is satisfied; a negative answer means it is unmet, incomplete, or unsupported by sufficient evidence.

The rubric pool supplies two-level categories and criteria, covering both broad evaluation dimensions and finer standards. Section 01 shows task-specific questions; the figures below illustrate the handbook structure and an abbreviated pool.

We built the handbook in three stages. First, we organized evaluation experts’ experience into initial rules and a rubric pool using the framework above. Second, we applied the initial handbook in other settings, such as RL training. RL scoring feedback exposed requirements and failure modes not yet covered by evaluation, including geometric-structure criteria. This stage expands the pool’s coverage across evaluation, RL, and other settings. Third, we introduced a strong external model to automatically refine the rubric pool and give the generator more precise guidance.

The third stage is an Agent framework for automatically refining the handbook. We treat rubric-pool iteration as a text optimization process inspired by SGD: a strong external model analyzes errors in production-task data, proposes discrete text edits, and validates the updates through actual inference. The number of rubrics it may modify per round acts as a step-size-like control; the process neither differentiates through text nor updates model parameters.

We prepare a training benchmark, a validation set, and an OOD dataset. The training benchmark includes production evaluation explanations describing why each sample received its label. In each round, the generator uses the current handbook to produce task-specific rubrics for the training benchmark, and the answering model judges them individually. The strong external model compares predictions with expert labels and explanations, identifies problems in the pool, and applies four operations: Add missing dimensions or criteria; Remove unimportant or inapplicable entries; Merge semantically redundant entries; and Split overly broad criteria into finer, independently answerable atomic checks.

The edit budget controls the size of each update. For example, a round may initially modify at most 5 rubrics. If 5 consecutive rounds fail to improve the target metric, the budget can expand to 10 or 15 rubrics to explore larger updates. Each candidate handbook passes through the full generation–verification–evaluation pipeline, including a generation-success check. The strong model continues to edit the pool using training-benchmark errors and production explanations, with the goal of transferring improvements to validation and OOD data—for example, increasing their mean accuracy, rather than improving training performance alone.

After introducing automatic refinement in the third stage, the handbook showed three main changes compared with the earlier version:

Three changes to the Rubric Handbook

  1. A larger question budget: 12 → 14 rubrics per case. The higher maximum gave the generator more room to cover important requirements with separate, atomic checks.
  2. Finer criteria for attributes, actions, text, and audio. For example, person-attribute criteria were divided into facial features and clothing, while action requirements were divided into order and pacing. Each rubric became more atomic, avoiding the combination of distinct requirements in one question.
  3. A new rubric: [Editing Quality][Overall Visual Quality]. This added an explicit check on the overall visual appearance of the edited result, alongside checks on the requested edits and local defects.

Without training any model, replacing generic fixed evaluation context with PAR context and then automatically refining the handbook improved the same zero-shot verifier’s Bench1 accuracy from 63.6% to 71.2%, then to 73.2%:

A well-specified handbook makes the generator more robust

We next examined the generator’s output. Human evaluation experts reviewed 1,000 samples and more than 9,000 rubrics, labeling each rubric as fully relevant, partially relevant, or unrelated to its task. Approximately 94% were fully relevant. Relevance varied across categories, with audio editing remaining a weaker area.

Direct GRPO, expert labels, and shortcut learning

The answering model receives the user instruction, the original and edited videos, and N rubrics. It directly outputs an N-position binary string, with each position answering one rubric. We skipped SFT and trained directly with GRPO because the training data was limited, creating an overfitting risk, and testing showed that the base model already knew the required output format. GRPO could therefore focus directly on judgment quality.

We designed two reward types. The format reward requires the output length to match the rubric count and every position to contain only 0 or 1, meaning unsatisfied or satisfied, respectively. The other reward measures accuracy. The accuracy-related term for rollout i and rubric j is:

\[R_{i,j}=0.1+0.9\,h_{i,j}\,p_{\mathrm{old}}(y_{i,j}),\qquad h_{i,j}\in\{-1,+1\}.\]

Here, h is positive for a correct verdict and negative otherwise; p is the probability of the emitted token. The format-to-accuracy emphasis was 1:9, and rubrics were equally weighted in this verifier setup.

Data design mattered as much as the loss. The original training set contained approximately 11k samples and was dominated by mixed pass/fail sequences. Only about 5% were all-pass and 0.4% were all-fail. The verifier could learn a shortcut: a long list of rubrics probably contains a failure.

The first augmentation removed failed checks from selected samples, retained a subset of passing checks, and shuffled their order. The next version further balanced all-pass, mixed, and all-fail examples, while reducing correlations between rubric count and labels.

Gold-Rescue: a learning signal for all-incorrect groups

For each prompt, ordinary GRPO samples eight rollouts (K = 8) and computes group-relative advantages per rubric. A difficult rubric may be answered incorrectly in all K. Our Gold-Rescue trigger operates on that rubric: when every sampled answer is wrong, inject one gold answer into the group and use it to provide a positive target.

For a binary correctness illustration, add one gold answer to K incorrect answers. With population-standard-deviation normalization, the incorrect answers receive advantage \(-1/\sqrt{K}\), and the gold answer receives \(\sqrt{K}\). This explains how rescue restores a learning signal to an otherwise uniform group.

Ordinary rollout tokens retain the PPO clipped loss. Writing the probability ratio as \(\rho_t=\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})/\pi_{\mathrm{old}}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})\), the per-token loss is:

\[\mathcal L_{\mathrm{on},t}=\max\!\left(-A_t\rho_t,\;-A_t\operatorname{clip}(\rho_t,0.8,1.28)\right).\]

The gold token is evaluated under a gold prefix. Following the policy-shaping function in LUFFY: Learning to Reason under Off-Policy Guidance, we use \(f(p)=p/(p+\gamma)\). With the gold teacher probability treated as 1, the corresponding loss term is:

\[p_t=\pi_\theta(y_t^*\mid x,y_{<t}^*),\qquad\mathcal L_{\mathrm{gold},t}=-A_t^{\mathrm{gold}}\frac{p_t}{p_t+\gamma},\quad\gamma=0.1.\]

For a positive gold advantage, minimizing this loss increases the probability of the correct token. The saturating transformation reshapes the off-policy update; it is not the usual negative-log-likelihood objective. Gold-Rescue adapts this ingredient to a targeted rubric-level trigger, rather than reproducing the complete LUFFY training procedure.

On Bench1, Gold-Rescue improved the V2 verifier from 81.4% to 82.7% (+1.3 percentage points), without collecting additional source training examples.

PAR for RL. A good verifier can also serve as a reward model for video-generation post-training, connecting data synthesis, RL training, and evaluation through a shared set of criteria. PAR uses the same task-specific checks to filter synthesized data, construct training rewards, and evaluate generated results.

Our local experiments indicate that PAR achieves higher judgment accuracy than conventional reward models, and that this improvement can translate into better downstream generation responses to editing instructions. So far, this finding is limited to RL methods whose policy-gradient contribution is scaled in proportion to a reward or advantage; it does not establish the same benefit for every RL algorithm.

Category-level rubric weights offer another control: increasing the weight on instruction following, preservation, or quality changes the emphasis of the reward, steers the generator’s optimization, and adjusts the relative importance of evaluation dimensions.

03 / FROM ONE MODEL TO A SYSTEM

Agentic PAR: multi-tool integration and harness design

Deployment exposes a different kind of failure

A more informative question can elicit an existing capability. It cannot guarantee that the capability is strong enough. After deployment, new data exposed weaknesses in audio preservation and editing quality.

A representative failure pattern is a visual-only object replacement that also changes the soundtrack. A verifier focused on the visible instruction may accept the edit while overlooking the audio-preservation failure. This suggested a targeted intervention: add an audio-consistency specialist for tasks whose sound should remain unchanged.

From business failures to targeted specialist tools

In a real production setting, expert evaluation explanations tell us why an edit failed, while its task-specific rubrics tell us which requirement the answering model missed. Together, they make it possible to locate a concrete capability gap—for example, failing to detect changed background audio when the task only requested a visual edit.

A practical path to rapid deployment is to supplement that weak capability with an expert operator. The iteration loop runs the current verifier, analyzes business explanations and error cases, locates the affected rubrics, then adds a specialist and adjusts routing before evaluating the updated system. This expands a single answering model into a model supported by a pool of specialist tools.

Expanding Step 3: operator discovery and validation. We searched for suitable operators through local sandbox experiments. A focused validation set contained 50 abnormal and 50 normal cases. A strong model proposed and executed candidate operators in the sandbox; candidates were assessed on both failure-detection rate and false-rejection rate. Only operators that detected the targeted failures while preserving valid samples were selected for integration into the fixed routing workflow.

For audio consistency on visual_only tasks, the sandbox investigation identified complementary signal-level checks. The configuration used the following thresholds:

Signal-level checkThreshold
Waveform correlation≥ 0.15
Spectral cosine similarity≥ 0.65

Residual signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)≥ 0 dB

These are configuration values for this audio-preservation setting. The check applies when the editing instruction requires the original audio to remain unchanged. A task that explicitly requests new dialogue or music needs checks matched to that request. The same discovery process can add a Lip-Sync Verifier for synchronization failures and a Visual Quality Specialist for visual defects.

Agentic PAR V1: specialists in a fixed workflow

Agentic PAR V1 put these capabilities into a fixed workflow: use applicable preservation prechecks, run PAR verification, and route relevant rubric types to specialists. The task matters: an audio-preservation check should not reject a sample merely because the user explicitly requested an audio change.

Agentic PAR V4: an evidence-based harness

V4 explored how to use a larger tool pool more effectively. Its harness has three stages. Rubric gap review checks for omissions and adds task-relevant synthetic rubrics. Tool orchestration binds checks to tools and collects evidence bundles. Evidence-based judgment makes a decision for each rubric, with an independent adjudicator when evidence conflicts.

Two advantages of the PAR architecture

1. Locate and supplement specific weaknesses. Business evaluation explanations, paired with task-specific rubrics, identify the requirements that the current answering model handles poorly. Specialists can then target those gaps, making the system easier to extend as new failure modes appear.

2. Build orchestration from explicit rubric-to-tool bindings. Each rubric can be bound in advance to multiple relevant tools. Those bindings and their associated rules define how evidence is collected and handled by the workflow harness. We did not first collect an expensive tool-calling training dataset or train a general autonomous tool-use policy. The system’s flexibility comes from extensible tools, task-specific checks, and evidence handling; it can be extended without requiring that tool-calling training stage.

04 / UNDERSTANDING THE GAINS

Why PAR is a better choice

Better context activates atomic capabilities

The first source of improvement is explicit task context. A rubric identifies what to inspect: a target object, a color change, a preserved background, or a synchronization requirement. Instead of asking the verifier to discover every relevant test on its own, the generator supplies expert-informed hints.

The handbook study—63.6% → 71.2% → 73.2%—is consistent with this mechanism. It shows useful headroom in how the evaluation problem is specified. It does not imply that every remaining failure can be fixed by prompting.

Training aligns the decision rule with experts

The second source is alignment with the evaluation standard. Even after the right question is asked, models and experts may disagree about whether an edit is complete enough or whether a defect should cause rejection. Expert labels provide the decision boundary; balanced data discourages shortcuts, and rescue supplies supervision on difficult checks.

The training comparison reflects the full expert-aligned setup, including data design and optimization. Gold-Rescue further improves the V2 verifier from 81.4% to 82.7%. Together, the context and training results explain why PAR improves both the questions and the answers.

They also explain its ceiling. A well-posed question cannot make an invisible detail visible, and an expert-aligned decision rule cannot reliably use evidence the model fails to perceive. That is where the answering process itself needs to become more capable.

05 / LIMITATIONS AND NEXT STEPS

The future of PAR: a hybrid System 1 and System 2 verifier

The rubric is present, but the answer is wrong

Among 186 remaining failures analyzed, 15 involved missing rubrics, 162 had a relevant rubric but an incorrect tool/verifier judgment, and 9 involved evidence aggregation. In this subset, improving the question inventory alone would leave the main bottleneck untouched.

Quality breakdowns: local details and transient defects

Quality breakdowns illustrate a capability that direct binary verification can struggle to cover. Two patterns explain why. Spatially local defects can hide inside an otherwise acceptable frame: the overall scene looks plausible, while a small face, hand, prop, or object part is malformed. Transient defects can appear in one frame and disappear immediately afterward: recognizing the problem requires comparing how a region changes over time.

Both patterns call for a more deliberate answering process: locate the suspicious region, crop or zoom into it, inspect neighboring frames when necessary, and then decide whether the artifact is real. The following fictional examples separate these two patterns.

A diagnostic experiment with tool-assisted reasoning

We then compared artifact detection on 63 confirmed editing-artifact cases from Bench5. Direct binary verification detected 14.3%, the quality specialist 42.9%, and the basic artifact detector 41.3%. These baselines motivated two questions: is the model failing to recognize a small defect in the available evidence, or failing to find the frames that contain it?

Targeted system prompt (SP). The three inspection configurations below—5 FPS without CoT, 5 FPS with CoT, and manually selected frames with CoT—use an SP specifically optimized for quality-artifact detection. The CoT configurations add explicit reasoning and crop-based inspection. The comparisons therefore concern an artifact-focused inspection setup.

At the same 5 FPS sampling rate, CoT with crop-based inspection helps the model examine the local evidence and recognize more defects. This supports strengthening the answering process after the rubric has already specified what to inspect.

Manually selected frames with CoT performed 3.2 percentage points below uniform 5 FPS. In this diagnostic setup, targeted frame selection added no benefit, while CoT with crop-based inspection improved detection. The results point to recognizing detailed local evidence as the main bottleneck, rather than finding the key frames: uniform 5 FPS was sufficient for this comparison. The useful next capability is closer inspection of what is already visible.

Let PAR define the checks; let the answering process adapt

We propose a hybrid answering system organized around what the base answering model can already do. For rubric categories already covered by its capabilities, expert-aligned training can teach a reliable decision rule; these checks can use System 1 for fast, direct binary verification.

For categories that remain difficult for the base model—such as recognizing a defect in a small local region—PAR can instead invoke System 2: more elaborate chain-of-thought reasoning combined with crop, zoom, or neighboring-frame inspection. The rubric specifies the task requirement; the answering path supplies the process needed to verify it. Temporal inconsistencies or conflicting evidence can likewise call for this more deliberate process.

Both paths can end in the same binary verdict. The distinction is the process used to obtain the evidence, not whether the final answer is 0 or 1. PAR supplies a stable interface for that process: a task-grounded question, the evidence needed to answer it, and a result that can feed evaluation, filtering, or reward construction.

APPENDIX / ANSWERING-MODEL CALIBRATION

Jev-style RLCD: making rubric scores less overconfident

From a binary verdict to a useful reward probability

Like a Jev-style verifier, PAR’s answering model can provide both a binary verdict and a probability that a rubric is satisfied. That probability matters when rubric scores are aggregated for filtering or used as rewards for RL. A model that assigns nearly 1.0 to uncertain or incorrect judgments can produce an inflated reward even when its output format and decision rule are correct.

We explored an RLCD-inspired calibration variant for answering-model training, using a Brier-based probability correction alongside the GRPO setup. The experiment used a Brier-inspired implementation rather than an official reproduction of Jev’s RLCD training recipe, which was not publicly available in the experiment record.

Brier-based correction reduces pressure toward extreme confidence

For a binary rubric label \(y\in\{0,1\}\) and a predicted satisfaction probability \(p\), the Brier loss is:

\[\mathcal L_{\mathrm{Brier}}=(p-y)^2,\qquad -\tfrac12\frac{\partial\mathcal L_{\mathrm{Brier}}}{\partial p}=y-p.\]

The experiment summarized its probability-correction signal as \(y-p\). This is the correction direction associated with the Brier loss. When a positive rubric is already assigned high probability, the remaining push upward becomes small. When a negative rubric is assigned high probability, the correction is strongly negative. The intent is to reduce overconfidence, particularly confident false positives.

The population interpretation is simple. If \(q=P(Y=1\mid x)\), then \(\mathbb E[y-p\mid x]=q-p\), with a stationary point at \(p=q\). This explains the calibration objective; it does not by itself guarantee convergence of the full neural-network training procedure.

Answering-model results and confidence shifts

In a separate calibration experiment on Bench2, the GRPO-RLCD variant improved precision and accuracy while keeping recall close to the standard-GRPO baseline:

Rubric probabilities also became less extreme. The mean score decreased from 0.803 to 0.775, and the share of scores in [0.95, 1.00] decreased from 75% to 67%, with fewer inflated predictions near 0.999.

The practical motivation is to make rubric probabilities more useful as reward signals. This training direction complements the main architecture: expert alignment improves the decision rule, System 2 improves difficult inspection, and probability correction targets overconfident scores.
